## Treat the forgiven balance as a separate figure

An income-driven repayment plan can cancel the remaining federal student loan balance after the borrower meets the plan's requirements. The amount cancelled is not a monthly payment. It can matter to your tax return. The IRS Taxpayer Advocate Service explains that the broad temporary federal exclusion applied to qualifying cancellations through 2025. Starting in 2026, balances discharged through ordinary income-driven forgiveness are generally treated as cancellation-of-debt income for federal purposes. A different tax exception may apply to particular borrowers or kinds of discharge.

Paying tax and repaying the loan are separate cash flows. When the calculator estimates what you will pay under a plan, it adds scheduled loan payments. It displays the potentially forgiven balance separately and excludes tax. A projected balance far in the future cannot tell you what tax law will say in the year of discharge or what your income will be that year.

## Check which type of forgiveness applies

Public Service Loan Forgiveness is generally federally excluded under the service-based student loan exception. Ordinary IDR forgiveness follows different rules. Other discharge pathways can have their own treatment. For example, when reading IRS guidance, look for the discussion of the kind of loan, the type of discharge and the relevant year. Do not copy the tax treatment from a borrower who used a different program or had a different discharge date.

State income tax can follow different rules. Use your state's tax authority to check how it treats the same discharge in the same year. A state exemption or absence of a state income tax is separate from a federal exclusion. Ask about the impact of a move if you lived in more than one place during the year. Keep track of your actual dates of residence instead of using the address on the most recent correspondence as your only record.

## Compare taxes as a range

Start with the amount of debt the plan might forgive. Record the year in the estimate and the projected income when that happens. Compare several planning cases that vary your income and the forgiven balance. The purpose is to see whether a future tax bill could affect the comparison today. The final calculation may change with brackets, deductions, credits and exclusions. A simple multiplication can illustrate sensitivity, but it does not determine a return.

A result that shows more forgiven can reflect a longer time with little principal paid. Look at the scheduled payments, interest paid, borrower-paid principal and amount forgiven together. If two plans have similar cash costs today but very different future balances, repeat the exercise at different future earnings. Ask whether a plan would end earlier if your earnings rise. A rise can increase monthly bills and reduce how much is left to discharge.

## Gather documents early

A servicer's discharge letter can show which balance was cancelled and the date it occurred. Keep this with your payment history and any tax form you receive. Compare the amounts and dates with the account. If the tax documents seem inconsistent, ask for a correction and retain the original correspondence. Keep a copy of instructions used in the tax year you actually file.

Some borrowers may qualify for an exclusion such as insolvency. Publication 4681 explains cancellation-of-debt concepts and possible exclusions. Eligibility depends on facts and documentation. A borrower who is unsure should gather details of assets and liabilities and consult a qualified tax professional. A balance on the student loan alone does not establish what belongs on your return.

## Plan the cash flow you can control

If discharge is near, confirm the remaining qualifying months and what the servicer expects the discharge date to be. Then discuss how a possible one-time income item could change your withholding or estimated payments with a qualified professional. If the discharge is far away, update your assumptions occasionally instead of treating today's tax rules as permanent. Include the months or years that are likely to make the largest difference in your comparison.

For PSLF, keep a record of the application and discharge type even if you expect a federal exclusion. For other IDR discharge, keep a copy of the current rule and ask about state treatment. A good planning worksheet is explicit about the source year, the borrower’s expected circumstances, and which amounts still need confirmation.

## Records to keep

Keep a plain-language record of the type and likely date of your discharge. Include the date you checked it and the source you used. If the information changes, retain both versions and note which one came first. This makes a call with a servicer or reviewer easier because you can explain the exact point that changed.

Next, write down the projected balance, expected income, and jurisdictions involved. Include these details in your own copy of the example and check both against the next statement. If a figure comes from a forecast, mark it as an assumption. Finally, identify the tax-year guidance or missing document to verify before filing. You can use that item as the next question if the answer still depends on someone else's records.

## A practical time horizon

A borrower far from an estimated discharge year can revisit the question periodically and whenever the plan or household income changes. A borrower nearing the expected discharge should spend more time verifying the actual loan balance, the date of discharge and the category the servicer recorded. Ask how to obtain a replacement confirmation if the account is transferred or later closes. If the amount changes between the estimate and the discharge letter, use the dated documentation from the discharge for your tax discussion and keep the earlier estimate to explain which assumptions were used.
