## Use the debt at repayment entry

The Tiered Standard Plan uses a fixed repayment schedule. The term depends on the amount of Direct Loan debt at entry to repayment. The federal rule provides ten years below $25,000, fifteen years from $25,000 to below $50,000, twenty years from $50,000 to below $100,000, and twenty-five years from $100,000 upward. At a boundary the higher term applies. Ask the servicer which balances it used, especially if the balance has changed since you began repayment or if you have multiple loan groups.

A future borrower can use the expected amount at entry as a scenario. A borrower already in repayment should check the original repayment term and months remaining. Today's loan balance can be much lower than the balance that determined the tier. An estimate based on a new term may understate the bill for a loan with less time left.

## Compare the whole payment schedule

A fixed payment covers interest and principal over time. Early in the schedule, more of the bill tends to cover interest, because the balance is larger. As payments bring the balance down, interest accrual normally falls and more of the bill goes toward principal. The nominal rate and term both affect the total paid. A longer term generally lowers a comparable monthly payment and increases the interest paid if you follow the full schedule.

The federal standard rule sets a monthly floor, with exceptions when the balance is below it. A very small loan or a larger payment can finish earlier than the maximum allowed term. For a portfolio with several rates, a weighted average can give a first impression, but individual loan balances and actual payment allocations are better inputs for a detailed estimate.

## Understand what changes with new borrowing

Borrowers with any Direct Loan made on or after July 1, 2026 generally face the new repayment choices: Tiered Standard and RAP where their loans qualify. A borrower whose Direct Loans all predate that point generally looks at the legacy fixed schedules and eligible income-driven options. Consolidation can create a loan with a new date. Before applying to consolidate or accepting another loan, ask how that new debt would affect options on the loans you already have.

The older Standard plan can have different terms for Direct Consolidation Loans. A servicer statement may use “Standard” without the details you need to infer the term. Confirm the exact plan and read the number of installments left. When comparing an older statement with a new calculation, make sure both use the same balance and meaning of the remaining term.

## Check forgiveness goals separately

Tiered Standard is not identified as a qualifying PSLF repayment plan. Legacy ten-year Standard may qualify under the PSLF plan test, but paying it on schedule from the beginning typically pays off the debt around the time you reach the PSLF payment count. A borrower with earlier qualifying payments may have a different outcome. RAP and eligible IBR can qualify when the other program conditions are met.

If public service is a serious goal, look up the certified count for each loan and ask if upcoming payments will be eligible. Compare the estimated balance at that date with the amount you would pay under each eligible plan. If your future work is uncertain, run a second case without PSLF. Capture the difference in the full payment path and the time you would carry the debt.

## Stress-test a fixed term

Review whether you could still make the fixed bill in a month when work slows or temporary costs rise. Use your emergency reserve and money left after essentials to decide how much timing risk you can carry. The lower fixed bill associated with a longer term may leave extra room in the monthly budget, but planned extra payments have value only when you can make them and the servicer credits them to the expected loan.

Ask your servicer to provide the current payment amount and explain what happens to the due date when you make an extra payment. Keep the first statement after any plan change. Compare it with your original estimate, including how much was credited to principal and how much to interest. If those differ sharply, check for existing accrued interest, a different term, the number of days in the statement cycle or a payment that arrived before the expected due date.

## Organize your comparison

Record your starting balance, rate and tier term next to the income-driven option's starting bill. Note which figures are contractual and which are scenario estimates. Check whether your employer might allow eligible forgiveness, whether your earnings are likely to change, and whether you would prefer a bill that changes with those earnings. The best fit can depend more on these questions than on the gap between two first-month bills.

A useful final check is to compare your balance after a few years, as well as the cost over the entire repayment period. Choose the schedule that fits your cash flow, then keep a copy of the actual term and due date so you can check the next statement.

## Understand what the chart can show

The first bill shows whether the schedule fits the current budget. The balance curve shows how much progress you could expect by the time you might move, take a new job or change another part of the budget. Because the line smooths daily accrual into monthly estimates, allow for normal differences between the plotted balance and each statement. Compare the first couple of statements with the schedule the servicer disclosed. If the figures still diverge, check the repayment entry amount, the separate loan rates, and whether all balances were included in the same plan. The answer may change the estimated tier and term.
