## Separate a filing decision from a loan estimate

Choosing how to file can change a Repayment Assistance Plan bill. It can also change tax credits, deductions and the total tax a household pays. The repayment estimator only sees the loan inputs you give it. For a complete comparison, you need a calculation of the loan payments for each spouse and the separate tax results. Start by considering scenarios you can actually use under the tax filing rules. Ask a tax professional when eligible filing options or dependent claims are unclear.

For joint filing, start with joint adjusted gross income. Do not add each wage amount to that number again. Wages and AGI are not always the same because the return may contain other income or adjustments. If entering each spouse’s share, make sure the values add up to the joint AGI you are trying to model. For separate filing, use the amount that would appear on the borrower's separate return. The borrower and spouse may need different loan or tax inputs in that case.

## Check which loans enter the ratio

Where joint income applies and both spouses have eligible loans, RAP can prorate a monthly amount by the borrower's share of combined eligible outstanding debt. The eligible loan total uses the balances relevant to the program. Private student loans can affect the household budget but do not belong in that ratio. Parent PLUS loans and certain consolidated loans can affect eligibility. Check the types shown by each servicer and ask which balances will be used.

The ratio can explain why simply adding a second income overstates one spouse’s bill. A borrower with a smaller share of eligible debts may pay a smaller share of the overall calculation. The payment floor still applies after proration. If both spouses are comparing payments, check both borrowers. A smaller bill for one spouse can coexist with a larger bill for the household. The [married borrower tool](/tools/married-rap-impact/) lets you change debt and dependency inputs to see how the estimate moves.

## Count dependents based on the return

RAP looks at tax dependents claimed on the return used for the payment calculation. Make separate entries for your joint and separate scenarios when the claimed dependents would differ. A support arrangement can matter for how you file, but entering a person in both spouses’ separate cases without supporting claims will distort the comparison. Use tax guidance for questions about who can be claimed and obtain help if support or custody circumstances changed.

An income-driven plan can treat family size differently. If you are also comparing IBR, check the separate IBR definition and regional poverty allowance. A particular person might change the RAP dependent adjustment and the IBR poverty allowance differently. Take care to check other qualifying supported individuals as a separate input when estimating IBR.

## Model a household budget, then a life change

Make two columns, one for the total joint-filing scenario and one for the separately filed scenario. Use rows for estimated payment for the borrower, estimated payment for the spouse, estimated tax, and any significant costs or benefits affected by filing status. Convert annual and monthly figures to the same period so they can be added. Keep near-term and longer-term totals separate if one payment is expected to change sooner.

Test a change in one spouse’s earnings, debt balance or employment. A debt ratio can change as one spouse pays faster or gets forgiveness. Some benefits depend on repayment timing, and future balances cannot be known exactly. A fixed spouse balance can make the long-term example easier to interpret while still leaving uncertainty beyond the starting payment.

## Prepare a question for the servicer

If your real bill differs, ask which tax return and AGI the servicer has, which dependent count it used, whether it included spouse debt, and which balances determined the share. Record the date the payment became effective and when you next need to recertify. Ask for a written calculation if the difference remains unexplained. Compare that breakdown with a saved worksheet so you can spot a missing loan or double-counted income line.

A different filing status can affect the rest of your finances. Talk through the combined effect before choosing a return solely from the payment difference on a website. An estimate is strongest when it shows every input and lets you replace the uncertain figures with documented ones as they become available.

## Records to keep

Keep a plain-language record of the filing scenarios, the claimed dependents, and each spouse’s eligible balances. Include the date you checked it and the source you used. If the information changes, retain both versions and note which one came first. This makes a call with a servicer or reviewer easier because you can explain the exact point that changed.

Next, write down the combined household bills and the tax estimate for each case. Include these details in your own copy of the example and check both against the next statement. If a figure comes from a forecast, mark it as an assumption. Finally, identify the largest unresolved assumption in the comparison. You can use that item as the next question if the answer still depends on someone else's records.

## Compare one variable at a time

It is easier to identify what changed if you hold all other figures fixed. First vary filing status, then check the result after adjusting dependents, and then test a change to the eligible spouse balance. If your expected AGI differs between the two tax returns, add those changes once the loan formula is understood. Do the same on the spouse’s side before adding the two loan bills. This is especially useful when each spouse has a different collection of loans, a different projected payoff date or a different forgiveness goal. Verify the actual numbers with the tax return and a recent account statement.
