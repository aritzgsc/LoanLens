## Know what changes ownership

Private refinancing replaces existing debt with a new private loan. The lender pays off the loans you select, and you owe the new loan under its contract. Refinancing federal debt this way can permanently remove it from federal loan programs. Federal Direct Consolidation is different: it creates a federal loan from eligible federal loans and can also change terms and eligibility. Before contacting a lender, identify whether your goal is a lower rate, a lower monthly payment, or a simpler bill. Different approaches solve different problems.

A lower advertised rate is the potential benefit usually highlighted in a refinance offer. A firm quote may depend on underwriting, income, credit, term length, autopay and other terms. Compare the actual offer with your federal loan statements. An ad or an informal rate estimate is not a signed contract. Read how the quoted interest rate, term and payment interact.

## Compare like with like

A lower monthly bill can come from a longer term and can cost more in total. A lower nominal rate with a shorter term can also produce a larger monthly payment. Use the same principal amount and comparable repayment length where possible. Include lender fees or other charges when comparing a written offer with your current debt. For variable rates, model a worse case as well as the initial quote.

Look at the current federal rate on each loan. A single quote can be lower than the rate on one loan and higher than rates on several others. If you are comparing a subset of loans, record which ones the lender would pay off and which ones you would keep. Check the monthly budget with both new and existing installments. Ask how any discounts would change if you turn off autopay or the lender sells your loan.

## List protections loan by loan

Federal loans can provide access to income-driven repayment, PSLF and other forgiveness or discharge pathways. Eligible borrowers may be able to request deferment or forbearance, depending on the facts. Some loans receive an interest benefit in particular circumstances. A private contract can offer hardship measures too, but those measures depend on the lender’s terms and can be materially different in scope and duration.

Review your likelihood of needing each benefit. Someone with uncertain earnings can place high value on a payment that adjusts after an income change. A borrower with substantial certified PSLF progress should check how much balance could be discharged if qualifying employment continues. Losing the program path can outweigh a quoted interest saving. Someone who can repay quickly, expects steady income and has no realistic use for those benefits may weigh the same offer differently.

## Test a harder month

Project a month with less income, a caregiving cost, or a temporary interruption to work. Compare the federal bill and the private contract's rules for relief. Ask how missed payments, fees, interest and credit reporting would be handled. Review the consequences for a cosigner if one is required. The goal is to know what options would remain if the optimistic quote scenario does not happen.

Compare a second scenario where the borrower's income rises and planned extra payments are made. This helps show whether the interest savings depend on carrying the debt through the full term. If you expect to pay the balance quickly, a small rate difference affects fewer months. If you expect a long period of repayment, check the total and look at how rate changes could affect a variable offer.

## Reach a decision using documents

Keep your current statements, plan terms, certified forgiveness counts and the written private offer in one place. Compare the exact amounts, expected term, fees and rate structure. Read the hardship rules, cosigner release terms if any and provisions for discharge. Ask your servicer about federal options and review your budget before transferring a loan out of the federal program.

The worked example on this page compares federal plans so you can see the baseline. Use a lender's own contract for the private payment and hardship terms. Put both sets of figures into the same worksheet and record any federal protection you would lose. If you cannot reproduce the promised savings from the written terms and your own balances, ask for the details before accepting.

## Records to keep

Keep a plain-language record of which specific loans the lender would pay off, and the current rate on each. Include the date you checked it and the source you used. If the information changes, retain both versions and note which one came first. This makes a call with a servicer or reviewer easier because you can explain the exact point that changed.

Next, write down the written private term, fees, rate structure and hardship policy. Include these details in your own copy of the example and check both against the next statement. If a figure comes from a forecast, mark it as an assumption. Finally, identify the protection whose loss would be hardest for your household to absorb. You can use that item as the next question if the answer still depends on someone else's records.

## Read the offer alongside a fallback case

A borrower comparing a lower fixed rate and a variable offer should write down how the variable portion can change, including the contract’s index and any maximums. Ask for the exact repayment and hardship terms in writing. If you are refinancing only some loans, check that the lender’s list matches the loans you intend to replace. Keep updated account access for any federal loans you retain and confirm the payoff status for each loan after closing. That makes it easier to catch partial payoffs and preserve any plan or protection you intend to keep on the remaining federal loans.
