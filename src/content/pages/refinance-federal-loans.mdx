## Who this applies to

This applies if a private lender offers to pay off your federal student loans and issue a new private loan. Federal Direct Consolidation is a separate transaction that stays in the federal program. Confirm which product is in the written offer before comparing it.

## What to check

### Gather the offer and loan terms

| Item | What to compare |
| --- | --- |
| Rate and APR | Compare with the federal rates and check whether the offer is fixed or variable. |
| Repayment term | Compare start date, number of payments and total projected payments. |
| Fees and conditions | Check closing charges, discounts, co-signer terms and when discounts end. |
| Borrower protections | Check relief for income loss or hardship, death and disability terms, and federal options you would give up. |

Use the lender’s written rate and term. A low advertised rate might not be the offer you qualify for. If the private rate can change, record when and how it resets and what limit applies. Compare the cost at the initial rate and under a higher-rate scenario. An advertised monthly bill can also look lower simply because the term is longer.

### Value the options you would give up

Check your eligibility for income-driven payments and any current PSLF progress. Refinancing federal debt into a private loan removes federal benefits for the loans refinanced. If your goal includes forgiveness, compare how many qualifying payments remain and how much federal balance could still be outstanding when you reach the target. Consider the effect of a period of lower income on your federal payment before choosing a fixed private bill.

If the offer involves only some balances, list what stays federal and what would be paid off. Run separate comparisons for those groups. Federal benefits that remain on loans you keep do not follow the loans paid off through a private lender.

### Compare the decision on equivalent terms

A lower interest rate can reduce interest if the same balance is repaid over the same period. Compare scheduled payments, fees and any projected federal forgiveness together. Keep the same date and balance for both estimates. The example shows federal outcomes only. Enter the written private offer’s total separately so you can compare it with what the federal alternatives would cost.

If the numbers favor refinancing only while employed at the current income, compare the private lender’s hardship terms with the federal options you qualify for. Get the terms in writing, including any rule that lets the lender change rates or stop temporary relief. Check the amount actually needed to pay off the federal loans before accepting a private loan based on an older statement.

### Simple comparison: the rate is not the whole decision

Imagine a **$20,000** federal loan at a **6% fixed rate**, alongside a hypothetical private refinance offer at **5% fixed** for the same term. The lower interest rate could reduce scheduled interest, but the private refinancing decision also removes federal options for the loans refinanced, including potentially relevant income-driven repayment, PSLF, and certain hardship relief.

Before treating a reduced quote as savings, obtain the lender's **written APR, fees, fixed/variable terms and actual payoff date**. Federal Student Aid explicitly warns that refinancing a federal student loan with a private lender can result in losing federal benefits. [Read the FSA financial aid dictionary](https://studentaid.gov/articles/financial-aid-dictionary/) and the [federal exit counseling guide](https://studentaid.gov/sites/default/files/exit-counseling-print-friendly.pdf).

## Common mistakes

- Comparing a promotional private rate to a federal rate without checking APR or fees.
- Treating private refinancing and federal consolidation as interchangeable.
- Ignoring certified PSLF progress when comparing total cost.
