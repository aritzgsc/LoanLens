## Start with the three tests

RAP is listed as a qualifying repayment plan for Public Service Loan Forgiveness (PSLF). The plan is one part of the test. You also need eligible Direct Loans and qualifying employment during the relevant months. Check the employer through Federal Student Aid's PSLF Help Tool, using the employer from your records. If you work for a contractor or through another employer, confirm which entity employed you before relying on the workplace name.

An income-driven bill and a qualifying payment have different meanings. To build credit, your payment has to meet the program's qualification rules for the month. A zero-dollar scheduled obligation under a qualifying income-driven plan may still count if the other requirements are met. A regular month where you skipped a billed payment does not become a zero-dollar scheduled obligation. Use your record to see which months were credited rather than inferring it from the balance.

## Check each loan's progress

The PSLF program requires 120 qualifying monthly payments. Employment and loan histories can vary over that span. One loan can have a higher count than another when it entered repayment earlier or had a different status. Enter the certified count for each loan separately in the [PSLF tracker](/tools/pslf-payment-tracker/) when the counts differ. Do the same in the calculator if you want separate repayment trajectories.

If your employer has changed, check both old and current certifications. A record that says eligible can mean the month meets the loan and payment requirements but is still waiting for your employment to be matched. Keep signed forms and confirmation messages. If you already meet the count, use the PSLF process and the current application instructions. Keep paying according to your servicer's directions until you know how your account and next bill will be handled.

## Compare plans using the same dates

RAP and IBR both can qualify on eligible Direct Loans. PAYE and ICR are identified as eligible through their transition period. The ten-year legacy Standard plan can also qualify, but a full schedule from the beginning often leaves nothing to forgive when the count is reached. Tiered Standard is excluded from the PSLF qualifying plan list. A low bill from a fixed schedule therefore cannot automatically be compared as if it builds PSLF progress.

Enter projected income growth, your earliest expected qualifying payment month and your existing certified count. Look at expected out-of-pocket payments through the count date, plus the estimated balance left to discharge. The calculator's savings measure compares the same plan with and without PSLF. It excludes taxes and other expenses. If an account will already be paid off, the estimate should show payoff rather than promise forgiveness.

## Be careful with time outside repayment

A period of administrative forbearance, deferment or a processing delay can appear differently in a payment count than in a balance statement. Check the exact dates and federal guidance for that status. Borrowers who were in SAVE should use the SAVE transition guidance and look up which months the program already credited. Do not assume that all months spent waiting for a new bill qualify.

In some circumstances, federal guidance describes a buyback pathway for specific months when buying them back would bring you to forgiveness. Compare the written eligibility conditions before adding those months to a projected date. A projection that counts uncertain months can make the remaining term look shorter than the record supports. Keep one scenario using certified amounts and another with unresolved months as a clearly labeled possibility.

## Account for a career change

If you may move to a different employer, run a second calculation without anticipated PSLF. The comparison helps you see the cost of staying with the same plan if future employment does not qualify. Consider whether a lower bill today makes the risks easier to manage, and whether the longer repayment under an income-driven plan creates a separate tax question. Both scenarios can be legitimate planning tools.

Your tracker is a calendar aid. To turn it into an account review, record the month of each employment change, the names on employer records, the count for every loan, and your next scheduled installment. Review it after an application, consolidation or employer update. Use the federal application instructions when you get close to the count.

## Records to keep

Keep a plain-language record of the certified count by loan and the dates of employment certification. Include the date you checked it and the source you used. If the information changes, retain both versions and note which one came first. This makes a call with a servicer or reviewer easier because you can explain the exact point that changed.

Next, write down the name and effective date of your next repayment plan. Include these details in your own copy of the example and check both against the next statement. If a figure comes from a forecast, mark it as an assumption. Finally, identify the first uncertified period that needs to be checked. You can use that item as the next question if the answer still depends on someone else's records.

## Review the final months loan by loan

If you are close to submitting for forgiveness, record which loan reaches the payment boundary first. An older loan can be ready while a newer loan still has payments to go. Confirm which employment periods need certification and whether your employer meets the eligibility requirements for those periods. Read the application instructions for your situation, ask how your account will be treated while the review is pending, and note which loans stay on their scheduled bills. A near-term calendar estimate is most reliable when it uses the individual certified counts and the date of the next qualifying bill.
