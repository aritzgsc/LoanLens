Two colleges can offer very different combinations of costs and financial aid. The school with the biggest scholarship isn't necessarily the one with the lowest out-of-pocket cost.

The [LoanLens college aid comparison tool](/tools/compare-college-aid-offers/) helps you compare offers using the **same academic year**. Use it alongside the actual financial aid letters and Federal Student Aid's [guide to evaluating aid offers](https://studentaid.gov/articles/evaluating-financial-aid-offers/).

## Step 1: Compare the same annual costs

Find the **cost of attendance** listed in each school's offer. Check what's included rather than comparing tuition alone. A school's estimate may include:

- Tuition and required fees
- Housing and food
- Books, course materials, and supplies
- Transportation and personal expenses

Living at home versus on campus can change the relevant costs. Make sure both offers cover the same period, such as one academic year, and similar living arrangements.

If a cost is missing or unclear, ask the school's financial aid office for a breakdown rather than inserting an unsupported estimate.

## Step 2: Calculate net price, not just scholarship size

For a straightforward first comparison:

**Annual net price = annual cost of attendance − grants − scholarships**

Grants and scholarships generally do not have to be repaid when their conditions are met. However, check whether they renew each year, require minimum academic progress, or apply only once.

Consider this example:

- **School A:** $30,000 annual cost, $7,000 in grants, $5,000 in scholarships → **$18,000 net price**.
- **School B:** $36,000 annual cost, $8,000 in grants, $11,000 in scholarships → **$17,000 net price**.

Despite its higher advertised cost, School B has the lower estimated annual net price in this example.

Federal Student Aid's [financial aid dictionary](https://studentaid.gov/articles/financial-aid-dictionary/) explains net price and other terms commonly found on aid letters.

## Step 3: Separate borrowing from the amount still owed

A loan can help pay a bill now, but it is **not a price discount**. You or another borrower generally need to repay it under the loan's terms.

Continue the example:

- School A: $18,000 net price − $5,500 in planned loans = **$12,500 left to cover**.
- School B: $17,000 net price − $4,000 in planned loans = **$13,000 left to cover**.

School A has the smaller immediate gap **after borrowing**, but the example includes $1,500 more planned debt than School B. Compare the amount left to pay *and* the loans you'll have to repay.

Use the [student loan repayment calculator](/calculator/) to explore a hypothetical future payment for the borrowing you expect to accept.

## Step 4: Look beyond the first-year offer

Before deciding, ask each school:

1. **Renewal:** Which grants and scholarships repeat next year? What conditions apply?
2. **Changes:** Could tuition, housing, or fees rise after the first year?
3. **Work-study:** Is the amount included in the offer earned over time rather than paid upfront?
4. **Borrowing:** Are the listed loans federal, private, parent loans, or some combination? Who is responsible for repaying each?
5. **Fit:** Are your academic program, transfer credits, and likely time to graduation comparable?

Work-study should not be treated as an automatic discount to the bill: it is usually earned through employment. Federal Student Aid recommends examining aid types and out-of-pocket costs rather than assuming every dollar in an offer works the same way.

## Five-minute college aid checklist

- [ ] Both offers cover the same academic year.
- [ ] Housing and meal arrangements are comparable.
- [ ] Costs, grants, and scholarships are separated.
- [ ] Net prices are compared **before** loans.
- [ ] Planned borrowing and the remaining cash gap are shown separately.
- [ ] Renewal conditions are confirmed with each school's financial aid office.

## Common questions

### Is the school with the largest scholarship always cheaper?

No. A larger scholarship can be outweighed by a higher annual cost of attendance. Compare each school's estimated net price.

### Does accepting a loan reduce my college's net price?

No. The loan may reduce the amount you must fund immediately, but it remains borrowed money with repayment obligations. Keep net price separate from the after-loan gap.

### Can I compare work-study the same way as a grant?

Not exactly. Work-study is normally earned by working, while a grant is generally aid that does not have to be repaid when conditions are met. Ask when and how work-study funds are paid.

### What if the two schools use different labels?

Use the underlying definitions and ask the financial aid offices to clarify unfamiliar amounts. Federal Student Aid's [aid offer comparison guide](https://studentaid.gov/articles/evaluating-financial-aid-offers/) is a good starting point.

This tool is educational and does not verify a school's costs or financial aid award. Always confirm amounts and terms with the issuing college.
