## Find out which situation you are in

Doing nothing has different consequences for a borrower entering repayment, someone already on a fixed plan, and someone on a plan that is ending. Start by identifying the current plan and whether your loans are actually in repayment. In your account, check whether you have a bill, a grace period, a forbearance, or an application waiting for review. Confirm the exact loan types and dates before assuming all the loans will move together.

If you are satisfied with an active plan that is not changing, you may only need to keep current with the terms of the plan. Income-driven repayment can require recertification or permission to use tax information. Watch for annual notices and record effective dates. A quiet month in your inbox does not tell you what plan the servicer will use several months from now.

## If your first repayment bill is coming

A borrower who never chooses a plan before entering repayment may be assigned a fixed schedule. The federal rules distinguish older loans under legacy Standard from new loans covered by Tiered Standard. The balance and loan history affect the applicable Standard term. Use the loan account's repayment disclosure to identify the scheduled term and monthly payment. Verify whether a consolidation loan or a new loan changes which group applies.

A fixed schedule may be acceptable if you can afford the bill and want a predictable timeline. If you intended to pursue PSLF, verify which plan payments qualify before relying on future credit. Tiered Standard is excluded from the qualifying plan list. Simply continuing on an automatic plan without checking that requirement can leave a different count than you expected.

## If you received a SAVE notice

Under FSA’s SAVE transition guidance, your servicer sends a first notice with 90 days to choose another eligible option. If you miss it, a final notice provides another 30-day period. A borrower who does not choose before the final deadline can be moved into a fixed payment plan. Your dates come from your notice. Use the [deadline tool](/deadlines/) to estimate a deadline only when the printed date is unclear, and confirm it with the servicer.

Open the next billing statement and check that the bill matches any account notice. If you cannot make the installment, contact the servicer about available relief as early as possible. Ignoring a bill is different from waiting during a forbearance or waiting for confirmation of a request. Ask explicitly whether anything is due now and what status the account will show while you consider your options.

## If you are still using PAYE or ICR

Federal regulations set the PAYE and ICR transition for July 1, 2028. Borrowers are told to select an eligible replacement before the transition. The rule describes default assignment to RAP for eligible loans and IBR for loans ineligible for RAP under the transition provisions. Check the applicability of the fallback if your account includes Parent PLUS consolidation or an unusual mix. The servicer can verify what would happen in your case if you do not choose a plan.

If you are close to an IDR or PSLF milestone, ask what happens to your existing credit. A record that combines loan groups may hide a different count on a newly consolidated loan. Consider whether the default assignment would keep the monthly payment manageable and whether its treatment of future payments fits your goal.

## Decide whether taking action is worth it

Write down the amount and due date of your current bill, the plan shown on the notice, and the text describing what happens if you do not respond. Compare that plan with another eligible plan in the calculator and run one scenario with a change in income. Review the difference in the first bill, projected cost and forgiveness outcome. Keep a third option on the worksheet if you expect a new loan or a consolidation soon.

If you do take action, save the confirmation and check the bill when the change becomes effective. If you decide to leave the account on the default schedule, set aside the expected payment and review your statements until the new amount settles. Neither approach requires guessing what a news headline means for your account. The next statement and the terms of your own notice give you the strongest record.

If a recent change made the bill unaffordable, ask which documentation you can use to report a changed income. Keep that request separate from a payment you already owe. Document the dates and amounts so you can distinguish a delay from a decision. That record also helps when you are close to a forgiveness deadline and need to verify which month was credited.

## Records to keep

Keep a plain-language record of the proposed default plan and the date it will take effect. Include the date you checked it and the source you used. If the information changes, retain both versions and note which one came first. This makes a call with a servicer or reviewer easier because you can explain the exact point that changed.

Next, write down the current bill, new bill and any pending application or documentation. Include these details in your own copy of the example and check both against the next statement. If a figure comes from a forecast, mark it as an assumption. Finally, identify the first task you would take if the fixed bill proves unaffordable. You can use that item as the next question if the answer still depends on someone else's records.

## If a transition changes your monthly budget

A borrower who can cover the expected automatic bill may still want to know when it starts and whether a second loan group bills separately. Compare due dates against when you get paid, especially if the first payment follows a long pause. A borrower planning to select a different schedule should compare the amount shown on the approval with the amount in the estimate before relying on it. Keep the date of the change and the next income recertification date together in your records. Both dates can affect the minimum amount you need set aside each month.
