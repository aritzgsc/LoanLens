## Start by checking which plans you can use

The Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP) and Income-Based Repayment (IBR) both connect your payment to income. They can give the same borrower very different monthly bills. Before comparing, check your loan types and whether any Direct Loan was made on or after July 1, 2026. Most borrowers with a new Direct Loan from that date have RAP as their income-driven option. Older eligible portfolios can often compare RAP and IBR. Parent PLUS and older non-Direct loans can materially change this picture, so check each loan's type and consolidation history.

IBR itself has two versions. The newer-borrower version generally uses a lower percentage and a shorter forgiveness schedule. The classification looks at prior outstanding balances, including qualifying earlier FFEL debt. An old loan that was paid off before later borrowing can make a simple first-ever loan date misleading. Confirm your classification if your history crosses July 2014 or includes a return to school.

## What each formula protects

RAP applies a percentage to your full adjusted gross income. The percentage comes from an income band, and the bill is divided into monthly installments before the dependent adjustment and floor. IBR protects a portion of income using a poverty guideline and applies its rate to income above that protection. IBR also caps the required amount at a Standard-based amount. A borrower with little discretionary income can have a zero IBR bill, while RAP generally has a minimum.

Dependents and family size mean different things in the two calculations. RAP uses dependents claimed for tax purposes. IBR uses its own family-size rules and a geographic poverty schedule. If you live in Alaska or Hawaii, the IBR figures can change for the same pay and household. A spouse can affect both calculations, especially when you file jointly and they have eligible student debt.

## Read the result in three passes

First, look at the bill you would need to cover during an ordinary month. Include irregular costs such as car repairs or medical expenses in your own budget. A plan that seems manageable today can become difficult after an annual income update. Use the income growth setting to run a second case before relying on the first result. If family circumstances might change, edit the household inputs and save the case separately.

Second, look at the balance trajectory. RAP can waive monthly interest that a full, on-time payment fails to cover and can provide a principal credit when the payment reduces too little principal. IBR can leave unpaid interest on the loan, with a limited subsidy on eligible subsidized loans. A lower IBR bill can leave a larger outstanding balance. A shrinking RAP balance is useful information even when the starting bill is higher. Your personal trade-off may be different if you expect a near-term job change or already have significant forgiveness credit.

Third, compare the calendar and the amount paid. A borrower expecting to stay in qualifying public service should run the calculation with a certified count. The best plan for reaching that milestone can differ from the best plan over a full career outside public service. Include a scenario where public-service work ends. If you have an old Standard schedule, ask what the real installment and remaining term are. A fresh fixed-term model using today’s balance is a reference point for comparison.

## Check the old credit before switching

IDR and PSLF counts answer different questions. For PSLF, you need eligible Direct Loans, qualifying employment and qualifying payments. For plan-based discharge, which past months transfer depends on the specific plan and the history of those months. An old payment labeled “IDR” in a spreadsheet should not automatically go into every plan’s prior-credit field. Ask for the count by loan, under the destination plan. If two loans have different dates of entry to repayment, keep the counts separate.

When you receive a proposed bill, compare it with the inputs used here. A mismatch can come from different AGI documentation, a spouse balance, a family member being included or excluded, the date of the first installment, or a cap tied to your history. Keep a copy of the confirmation and review the first statement after the plan changes.

## A decision worksheet

Write down the payment you could carry even on a bad month and the remaining amount you can save each month. Run the baseline at current income, a growth case, and a case with a change in household or employment. For each, record the first bill, years of payments and estimated forgiven balance. If you expect income-driven forgiveness, compare the cash you have available for possible tax with the projected amount forgiven. A large balance forgiven years from now has a different impact than an equivalent reduction in bills today.

Draw a line between facts and forecasts on your worksheet. Loan types, balances, certified counts, and a filed return can be checked. Earnings, household changes, future poverty schedules, and future tax law are assumptions. A clean comparison should make it easy to change one forecast at a time. Select the plan whose payment and risks you can live with, then use your servicer’s documentation to verify the choice and the effective date.

## A plan comparison with two different goals

Suppose you value cash flow today because your household is building an emergency fund, but you expect earnings to rise once a temporary contract ends. The cheapest plan in the first year and the cheapest over the whole projection can be different. Run the comparison at your current income, then test the later earnings separately and look for the year the rankings change. If you're considering a switch, confirm which old credits apply to the new plan and how your current accrued interest would be treated. Those two details can move the date by more than a small change in the first installment.
