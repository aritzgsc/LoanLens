## Find the income band first

RAP starts from adjusted gross income, or AGI, and assigns a percentage based on the borrower's income band. The assigned rate applies to all of that income. This makes a threshold crossing more noticeable than you might expect from a tax-bracket analogy. A small change in AGI at a boundary can change the percentage on the full amount. Look up your AGI on the income record your servicer is likely to use and check which side of the boundary it falls on.

RAP can use combined income for married borrowers who file jointly. When spouses have eligible debt, the payment can be prorated between them using eligible balances. Separate filers generally use their own income and dependents claimed on their own return. A person whose spouse has a large balance should check that the spouse’s loans actually count. Private loans are not part of the federal repayment proration.

## Convert the annual amount to a monthly bill

Multiply the AGI by the assigned percentage and divide the result into monthly amounts. Subtract the dependent reduction for each qualifying person claimed on the return. Apply the appropriate spouse debt adjustment where relevant and then check the payment floor. The monthly minimum can apply even when subtracting the dependent reduction would otherwise produce a zero or negative result. In the final installment you can owe less than the minimum if the remaining amount due is smaller.

Use the [income table](/rap-payment-by-income/) to see the band boundaries and test several dependent counts. The table uses sample income values. Enter your own figure in the calculator before comparing an estimate with a servicer statement. If your annual bill differs from what you expected, review the income used, which dependents were claimed and whether there was a spouse adjustment.

## Follow the payment into the balance

The bill and its effect on your balance are two separate calculations. A payment first covers eligible interest and applicable fees before principal. When a full, on-time monthly payment fails to cover all the new interest, RAP can waive the unpaid eligible interest for that month. RAP can also credit principal, based on the lesser of the regulatory cap or the payment you made, less the principal already paid by you that month. A minimum monthly payment therefore does not automatically translate into the full principal credit limit.

A balance chart can show how the subsidy affects the path. In a low-income scenario, the borrower may pay little interest out of pocket even though the loan would otherwise accrue more each month. In a high-income scenario, most of the benefit may be absent because the payment already covers the interest and reduces principal enough. For mixed-rate loans the chart uses a modeled distribution across accounts. Ask the servicer which account received the payment and credit if the real principal amounts differ.

## Update for annual changes

The initial payment lasts until the approved recertification or update changes it. Income may increase gradually, or a household event can produce a larger change. Model separate cases for first-year affordability and longer-term results. A one-time increase that pushes AGI above a threshold may have a different effect from a steady annual raise. An actual recertification can occur on a different calendar from your projection, so use the effective dates on the servicer's notice.

If your balance is already on another plan, examine any accrued interest and old payment history before deciding to switch. Ask which prior months qualify toward RAP's long-term discharge. PSLF uses a separate test. If you expect eligible employment, check the certified PSLF counts loan by loan and see the result with those counts. A plan ending because it pays off early can be good for a borrower who wants to be debt-free quickly, but it leaves no balance to forgive.

## Compare a real bill with a reproducible worksheet

For a repeatable calculation, note the AGI year, filing status, exact dependent count, balances used for any spouse proration, and the next repayment month. Save an individual case and a household case if you have two income earners. Ask what timing the servicer used when pulling the spouse balance. Review whether the current payment is the normally calculated installment or an amount adjusted for a smaller remaining balance.

When modeling extra payments, ask about the direction of the payment and whether it advances the next due date. Months with no payment due can change how an interest waiver or principal credit applies. Keep the regular installment and credit rules separate from the question of how quickly you can pay down the loan by choice. The calculator estimates the scheduled plan assuming on-time payments. Treat a strategy involving extra or delayed payments as a separate scenario to discuss with the servicer.

## Why two similar estimates can look different

A change in income can affect both the rate and the income it is applied to. In one scenario, a raise may leave the borrower within the same band, so only the income changes. In another, it may move the borrower to a higher band and change the rate applied to the whole amount. If your estimate looks jumpy, run the case just below, at, and just above the boundary. Then apply the same dependent and spouse assumptions to each example. This isolates the band effect from changes in household composition and makes the source of the higher bill easier to see.
