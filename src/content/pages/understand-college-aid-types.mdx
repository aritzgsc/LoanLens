A financial aid offer can look generous while leaving a large bill. The important question is not **“How much aid did I get?”** but **“What will school cost after aid that does not have to be repaid?”**

This guide walks through the four categories you are most likely to see in a United States college award letter. If two offers use unfamiliar labels, ask each school's financial aid office what the money is and when it will be available.

## The four kinds of aid

    <thead><tr><th scope="col">Type</th><th scope="col">What it means</th><th scope="col">What to check</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">Grant</th><td>Usually aid you do not repay, provided you meet its conditions.</td><td>One-year amount, eligibility and renewal.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Scholarship</th><td>Usually money you do not repay, subject to scholarship requirements.</td><td>Academic progress, program restrictions and renewability.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Federal Work-Study</th><td>An opportunity to earn money through eligible work.</td><td>How jobs are found and when you are paid.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Student or parent loan</th><td>Borrowed money that generally must be repaid.</td><td>Borrower, interest rate, fees, and repayment terms.</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

Grants and scholarships can have exceptions or conditions. **Do not count work-study as an automatic discount** from a tuition bill; it generally requires doing the work to receive the earnings. A loan may help you cover the bill now, but it is not free aid.

See Federal Student Aid's [financial aid dictionary](https://studentaid.gov/articles/financial-aid-dictionary/) for the official terminology.

## Example: a large award does not always mean a low price

Imagine one school lists this one-year package:

<div className="table-wrap" role="region" tabIndex={0} aria-label="Example annual college aid offer">
  <table>
    <thead><tr><th scope="col">Line on the offer</th><th scope="col">Amount</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">Tuition, housing, meals and other annual costs</th><td>$32,000</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Grant</th><td>$6,000</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Scholarship</th><td>$4,000</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Work-study opportunity</th><td>$2,000</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Federal student loan offered</th><td>$5,500</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

Start with **net price**:

$32,000 − $6,000 − $4,000 = **$22,000**.

If you decide to borrow $5,500, there would still be **$16,500** to cover for that year. The $2,000 work-study amount is not subtracted from net price because it must be earned and may not be available when the tuition bill arrives.

That does *not* mean you should borrow the full offered amount. It only separates the cost from the ways you might finance it.

## How to compare two schools fairly

1. Find the **annual cost of attendance** for the same school year. Include tuition, mandatory fees, housing, meals, books, transportation and personal costs where relevant.
2. Subtract **grants and scholarships**, noting conditions or one-time awards.
3. Record **loans separately**, including who will be responsible for repayment.
4. Ask whether the awards repeat next year and whether the cost of attendance is likely to change.
5. Discuss missing costs or unclear award terms with each school's financial aid office.

## Questions worth asking the financial aid office

- Is the scholarship guaranteed for all four years, or must I reapply?
- Does the grant change if my family circumstances change?
- Is a student loan listed in the package an *offer* or a required amount?
- Does work-study appear in the school bill, or will I receive wages after working?
- Are housing, supplies, insurance or transport missing from the estimate?
- What is the deadline to accept or decline each part of the package?

If the two schools have very different graduation rates, course availability or transfer-credit policies, those differences may matter more than a small first-year gap.
