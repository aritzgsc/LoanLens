Choosing a federal student loan repayment plan is about more than finding the smallest monthly bill. The amount you pay each month matters for your budget, but the time spent repaying, interest costs, and any forgiveness assumptions matter too.

Use the [LoanLens student loan repayment calculator](/calculator/) to compare estimates with the same inputs. Then verify available options using the [official Federal Student Aid Repayment Calculator](https://studentaid.gov/articles/repayment-calculator/). This page explains how to read an estimate, not which plan you personally qualify for.

## Start with four numbers

Before comparing repayment options, record these figures for each scenario:

1. **Monthly payment.** What is the estimated starting payment? Could future payments change with income, family size, or plan rules?
2. **Time to the end of repayment.** How many months or years does the projection cover? A lower payment often means a longer schedule, although the result depends on the plan.
3. **Total paid.** What is the sum of projected payments over the modeled schedule? This is different from the balance that remains today.
4. **Any remaining balance or forgiveness.** Is the estimate assuming a balance will be discharged? Does the scenario include rules or milestones you still need to meet?

Look at these together. A plan that is more manageable each month does not automatically cost less overall.

## Use consistent information for a fair comparison

A useful comparison starts with the **same loans** across all options. Keep your balances, interest rates, loan types, and starting date consistent when comparing plans. Otherwise, the results can differ because the inputs changed, not because the plan changed.

For projections that depend on income, pay particular attention to assumptions about annual income growth, tax filing status, dependents, and any previously credited payments. An estimate may look precise even when those inputs are uncertain.

Check your loan account or servicer statement for your actual balance, rate, and repayment history. Do not guess your eligibility from a simulation.

## Example: why the cheaper monthly option can cost more

Consider two *illustrative payment schedules*. They are not quotes or actual plan calculations:

- **Option A:** $300 per month for 120 months = **$36,000** total paid.
- **Option B:** $225 per month for 180 months = **$40,500** total paid.

Option B leaves $75 more in the monthly budget, but the simplified total is $4,500 higher and repayment lasts five years longer.

This is why comparing only the first monthly payment misses an important trade-off. In real plans, payments may change, interest can accrue, and qualifying forgiveness may alter what you pay. Always compare the full projections and their assumptions.

## Check how a repayment estimate might change

Ask yourself these questions before relying on a projection:

- **Income:** If earnings rise or fall, would the payment be recalculated?
- **Household:** Could filing status or household information change the estimate?
- **Loan type:** Are all loans eligible for the options you're comparing?
- **History:** Are previous qualifying payments, consolidation, or forgiveness milestones recorded correctly?
- **Dates:** Are there application, transition, or recertification deadlines relevant to your loan?

Federal Student Aid explains the [available repayment plans](https://studentaid.gov/manage-loans/repayment/plans). The details can change, so check the latest official instructions before taking action.

## A practical comparison checklist

1. Open the [repayment calculator](/calculator/) and enter the balances and rates shown in your records.
2. Compare monthly payments, total projected payments, repayment length, and flagged assumptions.
3. If you need a lower monthly payment, weigh the immediate budget difference against the long-term projection.
4. Repeat your comparison in the [Federal Student Aid Repayment Calculator](https://studentaid.gov/articles/repayment-calculator/).
5. Confirm account-specific eligibility and any required actions with your federal loan servicer.

LoanLens cannot access your loan account, approve forgiveness, or submit a repayment application. Its results are planning estimates.

## Common questions

### Does a lower interest rate always mean a lower payment?

Not by itself. The balance, repayment period, and payment structure also affect a projected monthly amount. Compare all inputs, not one number in isolation.

### What if my payment is expected to change each year?

A changing payment means the first monthly estimate does not describe the whole schedule. Review how the projection changes over time, and consider more than one possible income scenario.

### Does a projected forgiven balance mean I qualify?

No. A modeled result cannot confirm program eligibility, qualifying employment or payment counts, or an individual servicing record. Check program requirements and your loan history with official sources.

### Where should I check an official repayment choice?

Start with [Federal Student Aid's repayment plan overview](https://studentaid.gov/manage-loans/repayment/plans) and [official repayment calculator](https://studentaid.gov/articles/repayment-calculator/), then confirm any application or account-specific question with your loan servicer.
